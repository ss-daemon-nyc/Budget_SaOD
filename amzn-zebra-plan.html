Same structure as the AMD build — two deep calls, one at the money, all in one expiration — but the income leg here is a weekly, sold and rolled every Friday. That turns one decision into roughly two dozen, and it puts a hard calendar rule at the centre of the trade: Amazon reports on a Thursday after the close, so the Friday weekly sitting on top of a print is the one you do not sell.

The shape

Three core legs in one expiration, and one short leg that gets replaced every week

The core is a 2–1 back ratio and all three of its legs share one expiration — March 2027. That is not a preference: the short call only cancels the longs’ time value if it sits in the same cycle. The weekly is a separate decision layered on top, sold against the second long call, and re-decided every single Friday.

Buy ×2  /  core2 × AMZN 230 CDeep ITM. On this chain the zero-extrinsic strike lands near 78Δ, not the folk-rule 70Δ — AMZN’s at-the-money time value at this tenor is large enough that two shallower longs cannot absorb it.
Sell ×1  /  core1 × AMZN 260 CATM, ~59Δ. Its fat extrinsic pays for the extrinsic in both longs. That is the whole trick, and it only works at the money.
Sell ×1  /  weekly1 × AMZN 270 C wklyOTM, ~20–25Δ, ~7 days out. Covered by the second long. Sold Monday, gone Friday, and skipped entirely on the week the print lands.
Net result≈ 97Δ  ·  defined riskBehaves like long stock between the strikes, loss stops at the debit paid, and the weekly turns a static position into a cash-flowing one.

Naming: ZEBRA = Zero Extrinsic Back Ratio, coined on tastytrade. Adding a shorter-dated short call against the second long makes that half a diagonal — what most desks will call a poor man’s covered call. The “zero extrinsic” is a design target for the core only; the weekly deliberately carries extrinsic, because collecting it is the entire point.

The ladder

Every Friday between here and the core, and the ones you give up

This is the part the AMD build does not have. A monthly overlay is one decision a quarter; a weekly programme is not one trade but a queue of them, and its arithmetic lives or dies on how many Fridays are actually available. Prints come out of the queue. So does the run-in to closing the core. Edit the dates below and watch the count move.

Earnings calendar

Amazon reports Thursday after the close. The weekly that expires the next day holds the entire gap with one session left to react — that is the contract the policy removes. Mark a date estimated and the neighbouring Fridays get flagged too, because an unconfirmed date that slides by a week silently re-arms the risk on a week you had cleared.

Both numbers are arithmetically correct and they describe the same dollars. The left one is the number that gets screenshotted; the right one is the number that tells you whether the premium is any good. The gap between them is leverage — and leverage is also why the left-hand denominator can go to zero while a share position merely goes down.

The trade-off nobody mentions

Zero extrinsic and 100 deltas pull against each other — except when they don’t. Driving net extrinsic to zero wants your long strike deeper; driving net delta to 1.00 also wants it deeper. On this AMZN chain they happen to agree at roughly $230, which is convenient and not a law — it is a fact about today’s skew. Re-check it on your own chain, and if they diverge, hold the zero-extrinsic line and accept 0.90–0.95 deltas. Extrinsic is the thing that bleeds; a few deltas are not.

The weekly-income illusion

A weekly programme quoted against the debit will always look extraordinary. The same credit measured against the $26k of stock the position stands in for is an ordinary covered-call yield. Nothing is wrong with the leveraged figure — it is just not evidence that the premium is rich. Quote yourself the right-hand number before you decide the trade is free money, and remember that the weeks you skip are not lost income, they are premiums you correctly declined to sell.

      Failure modes
      
What actually goes wrong, ranked by how often

    

    

      

        
RiskSeverityHow it shows upWhat removes it

        
          

            A weekly left on across a printBlocking
            The clearest case on the tape is Amazon’s own Q2 2026 report, Thursday 30 July. The stock went in around $234, jumped roughly 9% after hours, and closed the next session at $271.58 — about +16% with one trading day between the print and the weekly’s expiration. A 25Δ weekly struck 4% out of the money would have finished roughly $28 in the money against a credit near $1.75.
            The ladder rule, applied mechanically: no weekly whose expiration follows a print. Note what this actually costs — the core still profits on a gap like that, so the weekly does not blow you up. It deletes the best week of the year and hands you a forced decision on the Friday. That is the thing worth one skipped credit.
          

          

            An estimated date that movesHigh
            You cleared the calendar against 22 October, Amazon confirms the 29th, and the week you decided was safe is the week that carries the print. Nothing on your screen changes to tell you.
            Treat vendor dates as estimates until Amazon’s IR page confirms them. Run the ±1 week policy while the date is unconfirmed, and re-check it before each of the two Fridays either side.
          

          

            Weekly margined as a naked callBlocking
            You send the fourth leg and buying power drops by tens of thousands instead of rising by the credit. The risk engine did not pair it with your second long.
            Clear it at Gate 3 before funding anything. Send the weekly as a diagonal spread order referencing the long strike, never as a standalone sell.
          

          

            Weekly outlives the coreBlocking
            A late roll pushes the short past the core’s expiration. The longs expire, the short does not, and you are outright naked short AMZN calls.
            A hard rule with no exceptions: the weekly’s expiration is always earlier than the core’s. Stop DTE on the bench exists to keep you well clear of the boundary.
          

          

            Death by round tripsMedium
            Two dozen open-and-close cycles at a dollar or two a contract each way, plus a half-tick of slippage every time, quietly removes a chunk of a $1.75 credit that looked untouchable in a spreadsheet.
            Model it: the realization % input is where this lives. If the programme only works at 100% realization, it does not work.
          

          

            Delta melts on the way downMedium
            AMZN falls toward your long strike; the core stops tracking the stock because the long calls are shedding delta, and it does not participate in the bounce the way shares would.
            Understand it as designed behaviour — it is the same mechanism that caps your loss. Set the close-or-hold rule before entry, at Gate 7.
          

          

            Extrinsic creeps back into the coreMedium
            The core’s short leg stops being at the money, the offset stops balancing, and the position starts carrying real theta in one direction. A big run in AMZN does this while you are busy collecting weeklies.
            Re-run the gauge monthly. Adjusting the core mid-life is usually worse than closing and rebuilding it cleanly.
          

          

            Expiration-week assignment scrambleMedium
            Both core legs finish ITM, auto-exercise fires, and Monday morning you own an unplanned share position — possibly on margin.
            Close or roll the core at 21–30 DTE. Do not carry a 2–1 ratio into expiration week to save a few cents of slippage.
          

          

            Early assignment on a weeklyLow
            A weekly finishes ITM and you are assigned short 100 shares against your longs, converting a clean options position into a share position you did not plan.
            Structurally low here: the usual trigger is a dividend capture, and Amazon has never paid one. Confirm that is still true rather than assuming it, and close ITM weeklies rather than letting them run to the bell.
          

        
      

    

  


  

    Standing assumptions
    
Built for a bullish core view on AMZN over the life of the chosen expiration, using calls throughout. Every figure is per-share unless a dollar total is labelled otherwise; one contract represents 100 shares. The weekly income model assumes a constant credit at a constant strike distance on every sellable Friday, which reality will not honour — the realization % input is the place to be pessimistic about it. Commissions, assignment fees, and bid–ask slippage are excluded from the headline figures and are emphatically not trivial across two dozen weekly round trips. Nothing here is personalised advice; the numbers are yours to verify against your own chain and your own account.
